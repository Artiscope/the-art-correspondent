Clarke Reynolds: Braille You Can See

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Braille is written to be felt. Clarke Reynolds also wants it seen, in colour, at the size of a wall.

Most art comes with a rule: do not touch. Clarke Reynolds makes work that asks for the opposite.

From one eye to none

Reynolds is based in Portsmouth. He has said he knew at six that he wanted to be an artist, after a visit to a local gallery. He was already blind in his right eye. In his early thirties he was diagnosed with retinitis pigmentosa, a condition that narrows the sight that remains.

Euronews reported that he had worked as a dental model maker and had to give the job up as his sight got worse. He kept making art and changed how he did it.

Dots as a language

Braille builds each letter from a cell of up to six raised dots. Reynolds takes that system and enlarges it. The dots become painted circles in strong colours, set out in the patterns of real letters and words. Sighted visitors see a field of colour. Anyone who reads braille can read what it says.

Arts Help quoted the text of one piece, in which he introduces himself as blind and as a visual artist who uses braille as his artistic language.

So the work operates twice. It is a picture and it is a sentence, and different people get to it by different routes.

Meant to be decoded

His first solo show, at the Yellow Edge Gallery in Gosport in 2021, was called Decoding Braille, Decoding Me. He described two visitors, one visually impaired and one sighted, spending about three hours racing each other to decode the works.

London followed in early 2023 with a solo exhibition at the Quantus Gallery, and in 2024 the Envision Arts Gallery in Wichita gave him his first solo show in the United States, with braille workshops in local schools alongside it.

Bigger dots

The scale keeps growing. In Tallahassee, Florida, in 2022, a local station reported dots three feet across, each the size of a pavement square, spelling the word love.

Who it is for

Reynolds also works under the name Mr Dot, a character he uses with children. The Big Issue reported that he is campaigning for braille to be taught in schools, as the system passes its two hundredth year.

The argument in the work is plain. A gallery that only lets you look has already decided who its visitors are. Change the material, and the audience changes with it.
