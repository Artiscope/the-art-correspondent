Zara Muse and the Physical Language of Portraiture

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

She paints with a palette knife and a spatula, and nothing else. The result is portraiture you read almost as much by its surface as by its likeness.

People who write about Zara Muse tend to borrow their words from sculpture. Art Plugged, which interviewed her in April 2025, described thick layers, sculptural depth and saturated colour. That is not a flourish. It is a plain account of what happens when a painter puts down the brush.

What the knife does

Muse told Art Plugged that she works only with a palette knife and a spatula. The choice shapes everything that follows. A brush can draw a line and soften it. A knife lays paint down in flat planes, each with an edge and a thickness of its own. You cannot fuss with it. A cheekbone becomes a single loaded stroke, a shadow becomes a slab of darker colour set beside it.

So a face made this way is built more than it is drawn. Close up, it breaks into ridges and facets. Step back and the planes lock together into a person. The paint stands proud of the canvas and catches the light differently through the day, which is part of why these portraits hold their own in large rooms.

The person behind the name

Zara Muse is the working name of Alexandra Johnson. She has said the name gives her some privacy. Before painting she worked in finance and trading, a career she has described as intense and demanding. She later left that world and became a full-time carer for her mother, who was diagnosed with cancer in 2017 and died in February 2019.

According to the same interview, she began painting again during the 2020 lockdown, slowly at first and then with growing intensity. Her children and a cousin encouraged her to show the work in public. She is now represented by Grove Gallery in London.

Women, and what they carry

Her subject is women. Luxury News Online, which profiled her in March 2025, reported that she set out to paint women's faces and profiles across backgrounds and stages of life, with resilience as the thread running through them. She has also credited graffiti and street art with giving her a love of bold colour.

The best-known example is her portrait of Malala Yousafzai, a subject she chose for bravery and strength. Art Plugged reported that it sold at Bonhams in London on 3 April 2025 for £51,200 including premium. The same report lists earlier results at Tate Ward: £33,750 for In Her Own Light and £47,500 for The Shape of Stillness.

A language you can see being spoken

What makes this a physical language is that the surface keeps a record of its own making. Every stroke shows its direction and its pressure. Nothing is blended away. In a period when most images reach us flat and backlit, a portrait that so openly shows the work of a hand is saying something before the face has said anything at all.
