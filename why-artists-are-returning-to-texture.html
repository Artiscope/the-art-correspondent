Why Contemporary Artists Are Returning to Texture

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Most of the art we see now arrives on glass. That may be exactly why surfaces that cast their own shadows feel so alive.

We should be honest at the start. There is no survey that proves painters are turning back to texture, and we will not pretend to have one. What we can report is what we see in the work we cover, and offer an argument for why it matters.

Thick paint is old

Texture is not a new idea. Rembrandt's late paintings are heavy with built-up paint. Van Gogh pushed pigment around until the strokes became the subject. In London, Frank Auerbach spent decades loading and scraping his canvases until the surface stood out like relief. Painters call the technique impasto, and it has never fully gone away.

What a screen cannot carry

What has changed is how we look. A phone shows every artwork at the same size, at the same brightness and with the same smooth finish. A photograph of a thickly painted canvas keeps the colour and loses almost everything else: the depth, the weight, the way a ridge of paint throws a small shadow as the light moves.

Texture is the part of a painting that does not survive that journey. For an artist, that makes it valuable. It is a reason to stand in front of the work itself, and it is proof that a hand was there.

Two examples from our pages

Zara Muse, whose portraits we look at elsewhere in this issue, told Art Plugged that she paints only with a palette knife and a spatula. Her surfaces are made of planes and edges, closer to modelling than to drawing. The material is doing some of the talking.

Konn Artiss works at the other extreme. For ANTI-FRIEZE in October 2025, the artist left five blocks of ice outside Frieze London, each holding a small figure of a famous artist. Ice is texture with a time limit. It was cold, wet and heavy, and then it was a puddle. Nobody who was there needed a caption to understand the material.

A caution

Texture is not meaning. Thick paint can be as empty as thin paint, and a rough surface can be a habit as easily as a decision. The question worth asking in front of any heavily worked piece is a simple one: what would be lost if this were smooth? When there is a real answer, the texture has earned its place.
