Why Collectors Buy Faces

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

A landscape gives you somewhere to look. A portrait looks back. That may be the whole explanation.

Ask people what art they would hang at home and many will say a view: sea, hills, a street they love. Yet some of the most talked-about sales in contemporary art are of faces. It is worth asking why.

The oldest subject

For most of history a portrait was bought by the person in it. It recorded rank, marked a marriage or kept a likeness after death. The buyer and the subject were the same household.

Today that link has almost gone. Collectors buy portraits of strangers, of public figures, of people the artist knows and they never will.

What the market shows

The prices suggest the appetite is real. Art Plugged reported that Zara Muse's portrait of Malala Yousafzai sold at Bonhams in London in April 2025 for £51,200 including premium. Ocula records that Amoako Boafo's The Lemon Bathing Suit made £675,000 at auction in 2020, a year after it was painted.

Museums tell the same story. In 2018 the National Portrait Gallery in London showed Njideka Akunyili Crosby's series The Beautyful Ones. In 2024 the Belvedere in Vienna gave Boafo more than fifty works' worth of wall.

A caution is due. A handful of results is not a study of the market, and we do not claim that portraits outsell everything else. These are examples of demand, nothing more.

Three reasons

Why would someone want a stranger's face in the sitting room? We can suggest three reasons.

The first is company. A room with a portrait in it has someone else in it. People who live with one often talk about it as a presence.

The second is values. To hang a portrait of Malala Yousafzai is to say something about what you admire. Muse has said she chose the subject for bravery and strength. The buyer is choosing those too.

The third is recognition. For a long time the faces on gallery walls were drawn from a narrow slice of the world. Boafo turned to portraits of Black sitters after moving to Vienna, where by several accounts he found them largely missing from what was shown. For many collectors, buying such a portrait is a way of putting right what was missing.

The hand in the face

There is a fourth reason, which has to do with how these pictures are made. Muse builds faces with a knife. Boafo paints skin with his fingers. Akunyili Crosby fills hers with transferred photographs.

In each case the making is visible in the face itself. In a time when any image can be produced in seconds, a face that clearly took a person days to make has a pull of its own.

Looking back

The simplest answer may still be the best. We are built to look at faces. We read them before we can read anything else. A painted face, hung where you pass it every day, never quite becomes furniture.
