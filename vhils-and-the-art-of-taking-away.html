Vhils and the Art of Taking Away

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Nearly every picture ever made was made by adding something to a surface. Vhils makes his by taking the surface away.

On the side of a worn building, a face several metres high looks out from the plaster. There is no paint on it. The light parts are where the old render still clings. The dark parts are where it has been cut back to brick.

This is the work of Alexandre Farto, who signs himself Vhils. He was born in 1987 and grew up on the edge of Lisbon, across the river from the city.

A boy with a spray can

He started writing graffiti at thirteen. His gallery tells how the walls around him were already crowded. After the revolution of 1974, Portuguese walls filled with political murals, and in the decades that followed those were buried under advertising, posters and more paint. A wall in his neighbourhood was a stack of years.

He later studied in London, at Byam Shaw and Central Saint Martins. The idea he arrived at was simple and odd. If the wall is made of layers, the picture is already in there. You only have to dig.

Scratching the Surface

He gave the method a plain name. By his own account, Scratching the Surface was first shown in Lisbon in 2007 and reached an international audience at the Cans Festival in London in 2008.

The process starts like a stencil. He marks out the portrait on the wall, deciding what will stay and what will go. Then the tools come out: chisels, hammers, drills, jackhammers. Reports of his work list acid and small explosive charges as well. What is left is a face in low relief, made of the wall's own history.

Whose faces

The people in the portraits are rarely famous. His gallery describes them as anonymous city dwellers, and notes that the walls he chooses are neglected or abandoned ones. A person nobody would paint, on a wall nobody would look at. The pairing is the point.

He has a phrase for what he does: an archaeology of the present.

Beyond the wall

The same thinking runs through his other work. In the studio he peels and cuts into thick pads of salvaged street posters. He has etched metal, carved wood and made pieces with controlled explosions. In each case the image comes from removing material.

The carved walls now appear in cities from Moscow to Sydney, and he has shown in galleries in London, Paris and Hong Kong.

Why it stays with you

Most public art puts something new in front of you. A Vhils portrait shows you what was already there, under the paint and the posters. It treats the city as a record that can be read.

It is also honest about time. The wall will keep crumbling. One day the face will go the way of everything else that was ever on that surface. Until then it looks back at the street that made it.
