Portraiture, Celebrity and Contemporary Cultural Memory

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

A portrait of a famous face is never only a likeness. It is a bid for how that person will be remembered.

In 1962, within weeks of Marilyn Monroe's death, Andy Warhol made the Marilyn Diptych. Fifty repeated faces, half in bright colour and half fading to grey. More than sixty years on, it is hard to picture Monroe without picturing Warhol's version of her.

That is the power of the celebrity portrait. It does not just record fame. It fixes a particular image of it in the shared memory.

Who gets painted now

For most of history the answer was simple: whoever could pay. Kings, merchants, their families. The portrait was proof of standing. Today artists more often choose their own subjects, and the choice is itself a statement about who deserves a place on the wall.

Zara Muse chose Malala Yousafzai. She told Art Plugged the subject stood for resilience, bravery and strength. The portrait sold at Bonhams in London in April 2025 for £51,200 including premium. Set aside the price and look at the decision. A painter known for portraits of women picked a living campaigner for girls' education and gave her the full weight of thick paint and saturated colour. That is an argument about who our icons should be.

When the famous are artists

Konn Artiss turned the same lens on the art world. For ANTI-FRIEZE in October 2025, the anonymous artist froze small effigies of Damien Hirst, Andy Warhol, Tracey Emin, Jeff Koons and KAWS in blocks of ice outside Frieze London and let them melt.

Art Plugged read the work as a comment on artistic immortality. It is also, in its odd way, a group portrait. These five are among the few artists famous enough to be recognised as dolls. The piece asks how long that kind of fame lasts once the conditions around it change.

Memory is a choice

Put the two beside each other and a pattern shows. One artist builds a face up in paint so that it will last. The other builds faces in ice so that they will not. Both are working on the same material, which is public memory.

A portrait of a living person is always a little early. The story is not finished and the painter is guessing at the ending. That is what makes the form risky, and why it still matters. When you next stand in front of a famous face in paint, ask who chose it, and what they are hoping you will remember.
