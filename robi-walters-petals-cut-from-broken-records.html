Robi Walters: Petals Cut from Broken Records

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

He cuts petals from broken vinyl and old packaging, and arranges them into flowers that seem to burst off the wall.

Robi Walters was born in London in 1973 and still works there. His material is what other people throw away.

Rubbish, rearranged

The collages are made from discarded flyers, cardboard, household packaging and broken vinyl records. He cuts these into petal shapes, hundreds of them, and layers them outward from a centre. The Week described the effect as an explosive three-dimensional collage of colour, like fireworks or bursts of lotus flowers.

The lotus is not an accident. House Collective traced the work to sacred geometry and to the Buddhist symbol of the thousand-petal lotus, and to the calm Walters looks for in meditation.

A day with two ends

His routine has a shape. An artist profile published in 2017 notes that he meditates every morning and ends each day by making a painting, a kind of diary in paint. Cent Magazine reported where the habit came from: the Turner Prize winner Chris Ofili advised him to paint every day, and he took the advice literally.

So there are two bodies of work running side by side. The slow, exact collages, and the quick daily paintings that keep the hand moving.

Where it comes from

Walters has spoken openly about a difficult childhood. The Week reported that he was fostered and spent time in and out of children's homes. He told one interviewer that he cannot separate his early life from the work, and gave the recycled material as the example. Cent Magazine noted that he is now close to both his mother and his foster family.

House Collective describes him as self-taught. In that light the choice of material reads less like a style and more like a statement: nothing is beyond being made into something.

Recognition

The Week reported that at forty-four he opened a gallery of his own in Soho. More recently, Grove Gallery lists his work in its residency at South Place Hotel in east London.

Stand in front of one of the collages and you can still read fragments of the old life of the material: a groove, a scrap of lettering, the edge of a label. That is the point. The record is broken. It has become something else.
