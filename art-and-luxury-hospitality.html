When Art Enters the World of Luxury Hospitality

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Paintings are leaving the white room for the hotel bar. It is worth asking what they gain there, and what they give up.

A gallery asks for a certain kind of attention. You go on purpose, you stand, you look, you leave. A hotel asks for nothing. People sit for an hour with a drink and the painting is simply there, at the edge of the conversation.

A case in Windsor

In November 2024, FAD Magazine reported on a year-long partnership between Grove Gallery and the Fairmont Windsor Park hotel. Fourteen works from a new collection by Zara Muse were hung in the hotel's Bar 1215 and in its fine dining restaurant.

The launch was tied to a second partnership. Astrea London, which makes lab-grown diamonds, opened a boutique in the hotel, and Muse made a centrepiece portrait called Revolution that incorporates a 14.9 carat diamond.

It is not a one-off. Luxury News Online reported in March 2025 that her work also hangs in Kanishka, a restaurant in Mayfair, and that she had expanded into Dubai. Art Plugged described her paintings in hotel lobbies and private homes across Europe and the Middle East.

What the art gains

The first gain is time. A guest may spend longer near a painting in a bar than most visitors spend in front of anything in a museum. The second is audience. Many people who would never walk into a commercial gallery will happily spend an evening in a hotel.

FAD quoted the gallery's own view: that art should be accessible and part of everyday life, and that these rooms let it become part of the surroundings instead of a static display.

What it risks

The risk is the same thing seen from the other side. Art that becomes part of the surroundings can become furniture. Hotels choose work that suits a mood, and difficult or uncomfortable pictures rarely make the cut. A painting set beside a diamond is also, plainly, being asked to do a job for a brand.

Questions for the visitor

None of that is a reason to look away. It is a reason to look properly. Next time you find a painting in a hotel, ask three things. Is the artist named? Is there anything to tell you about the work? Could you see it without booking a table? The answers say a good deal about whether the art is a guest in the room or just part of the décor.
