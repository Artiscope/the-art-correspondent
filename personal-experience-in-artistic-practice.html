The Role of Personal Experience in Artistic Practice

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

A life does not explain a painting. But it often explains why the painting exists at all.

Ask an artist where the work comes from and the first answer is usually about materials or method. The second answer, if you wait for it, is usually about something that happened to them.

One account

Zara Muse has been open about hers. In an interview with Art Plugged in April 2025 she described caring for her mother through cancer, and her mother's death in February 2019. She said the loss changed her and made her more determined. She began painting again during the lockdown of 2020.

She has also spoken about what she left behind. Before painting she worked in finance and trading. She told the same interviewer that the old career does not feed directly into the pictures, but that leaving it gave her room to find herself. The portraits that followed are of women, and she describes them as a tribute to resilience.

None of this tells you whether a given portrait is good. What it tells you is what the artist was looking for when she picked up the knife.

A long tradition

She is in old company. Frida Kahlo began painting in earnest while confined to bed after a bus accident in 1925, and her own body remained her subject for the rest of her life. Louise Bourgeois returned to her childhood again and again across a career that lasted into her nineties. Tracey Emin showed her own unmade bed in 1998 and made the private life of the artist a public question.

The risk

There is a danger in all this. A moving story can be used to sell a weak picture, and a strong picture can be flattened into an illustration of its maker's biography. Viewers are right to be wary when the story arrives before the work does.

The opposite choice

Some artists refuse the bargain entirely. Konn Artiss, whose public interventions we report on in this issue, works anonymously. There is no childhood, no studio portrait and no origin story on offer. The work has to stand on what it does in the street.

Both approaches are honest in their way. One says: this is where it came from. The other says: that is none of your business, look at the thing. As readers and viewers, our job is the same in each case. Look first. Then, if the artist has offered it, listen to the story and see whether the work can carry it.
