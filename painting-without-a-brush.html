Painting Without a Brush: Knives, Fingers and Chisels

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

A palette knife, a fingertip, a chisel. Three artists who gave up the brush, and what each one got in return.

The brush is such an ordinary part of painting that we rarely ask what it does. It holds paint, it draws a line, it softens an edge. It also puts fifteen centimetres of wood between the artist and the picture. Some artists decide they want that distance gone.

The knife

Zara Muse told Art Plugged that she works only with a palette knife and a spatula. A knife cannot draw a thin line or blend gently. It lays paint down in flat pieces with clear edges. A face built this way is made of planes, and the paint stands proud of the canvas. The limit is the point. It forces decisions.

The finger

Amoako Boafo went further and removed the tool altogether. According to Colección SOLO, he began painting with his fingers in 2017. He does not do it everywhere. Accounts of his method note that the skin of his sitters is finger-painted in oil, while clothes and backgrounds are often handled with brushes and paper transfer.

So in a Boafo portrait you can see exactly where the hand has been. The body is alive with swirls. Everything around it is still.

The chisel

The Portuguese artist Vhils, born Alexandre Farto in 1987, does not add anything at all. He takes away. Using chisels, mallets, picks and jackhammers, he cuts into the plaster of old walls until a portrait appears in the layers underneath.

He calls the approach Scratching the Surface. His own biography dates its first public showing to Lisbon in 2007 and its international debut to the Cans Festival in London in 2008. The faces are usually of ordinary, unnamed people, and the walls are ones a city has neglected.

His gallery traces the idea to the walls he grew up with outside Lisbon, thick with decades of posters and paint. To carve into them is to read the history of the place.

What the tool decides

Put the three side by side and a pattern shows. Each tool leaves a different kind of evidence. The knife leaves an edge. The finger leaves a touch. The chisel leaves a wound that becomes a face.

None of this makes the brush wrong. But a change of tool is never only technical. It changes how close the artist stands, how fast they can work and what kind of mistake is possible. The picture you end up looking at is, in part, a record of that choice.
