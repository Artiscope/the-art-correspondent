When an Artist Is Also a Celebrity

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

A famous name gets the work seen. It also means most people have made up their minds before they look.

There are two kinds of famous artist. One becomes well known because of the art. The other is well known already and then begins to show art. The art world tends to admire the first and distrust the second.

Fame made by art

Andy Warhol is the model for the first kind. He treated fame as a material. He painted film stars, ran a studio that was also a social scene, founded a magazine and turned himself into one of the most recognisable figures of his century.

Britain had its own version in the 1990s. Damien Hirst put a shark in a tank. Tracey Emin showed her own unmade bed. Both became household names, known to people who had never been inside a gallery.

In these cases the celebrity grew out of the work, and nobody now doubts that they were artists first.

Fame that arrives first

The second kind has a harder time. Amanza Smith was known to television audiences from the series Selling Sunset before she showed a painting. When she made her debut at MASH Gallery in Los Angeles, the gallery described her as multifaceted and no stranger to reinvention.

Her canvases are large, layered and full of colour, with collage, graffiti-like marks and, in recent work, robotic forms. In April 2025 they were shown in Palma at the opening of a Grove Gallery space, beside work by Zara Muse and Silent Mike.

The suspicion such artists meet is easy to understand. Galleries want visitors, and a famous name brings them. So people wonder whether the work would be on the wall without it.

The opposite choice

It is striking how many artists run the other way. Konn Artiss is anonymous. Zen Xander's own website says the anonymity is there to keep the focus on the work and away from fame. These artists have looked at fame and decided it would get in the way.

So the same art world holds people who are known and want to be judged as artists, and artists who refuse to be known at all.

Looking fairly

How should a viewer handle this? Not by pretending the name is absent. You cannot unknow who made a picture.

But you can notice what the name is doing to your judgement. If you would walk past the work with an unknown signature, fame is flattering it. If you would stop for it and are dismissing it because of who made it, fame is costing it. Both are errors.

The fair test is the dull one. Give it the time you would give anything else. Look at the colour, the surface, the decisions. Then ask whether you would want to see it again.

A longer view

Time settles most of these questions. Celebrity fades faster than paint. In fifty years few people will remember who was on which television programme, and the pictures will have to manage alone. That is the real test, and it applies to everyone.
