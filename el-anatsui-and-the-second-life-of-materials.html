El Anatsui and the Second Life of Materials

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Flatten a bottle cap, punch a hole in it, wire it to the next one. Repeat a few hundred thousand times.

From a distance, an El Anatsui hanging looks like cloth of gold. It folds and ripples and catches the light. Walk up to it and you find it is made of rubbish: the aluminium caps and collars from liquor bottles, each one flattened and wired to its neighbours.

A bag by the road

Anatsui was born in Anyako, Ghana, in 1944, and has worked for decades in Nigeria, with a studio also in Tema, Ghana. The story of the bottle caps is often told. In 1998, walking near Nsukka in Nigeria, he noticed a bag of them by the roadside and took it back to the studio.

He began to experiment: folding the caps, slicing them into rounds, opening out the sides. With assistants he found a method that worked. The metal is pierced in several places and the pieces are linked with copper wire.

Many hands

The scale needs a workshop. Sculpture magazine described teams of assistants, recruited near his studios, who clip, flatten and link the caps into sheets. Those sheets are modules. Ocula's account of his process has Anatsui stepping in at the end to arrange them into the final composition.

He has said that the colours of the caps, the yellows, blacks and reds, led him to think like a painter as well as a sculptor.

Nothing is fixed

Because the sheets are flexible, the works have no single correct shape. They can be draped, bunched or stretched, and they are hung differently each time they are shown. The people installing them make real decisions. That is unusual for sculpture, and deliberate.

The Turbine Hall

The largest test came in London. For Tate Modern's Hyundai Commission, Anatsui made Behind the Red Moon, shown in the Turbine Hall from 10 October 2023 to 14 April 2024. It came in three parts: The Red Moon, The World and The Wall.

Tate's guide describes thousands of bottle tops and metal fragments crumpled, crushed and joined by hand. The first hanging, in red, formed the outline of a moon in eclipse and billowed like a sail.

Why bottle caps

The material is not neutral. Liquor was one of the goods traded along the routes that carried enslaved people across the Atlantic, and Tate's guide ties the work to those histories of power and survival. The caps are cheap, local and plentiful. They also carry a past.

That is the second life of the title. The object is saved from the roadside, and its history comes with it.
