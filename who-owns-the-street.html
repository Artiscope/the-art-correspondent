Who Owns the Street? Public Art With and Without Permission

By The Art Correspondent. Published 19 November 2026.

Some public art is commissioned. Some appears overnight. The interesting question is who gets to say yes.

A painting in a gallery has a simple chain of permission. The gallery owns the wall and chose the work. Step outside and it gets complicated at once. The wall has an owner. The street has a council. The neighbours have opinions. And the artist may not have asked any of them.

Three kinds of yes

It helps to separate three permissions that are often lumped together. There is the law, which decides whether marking a surface is a crime. There is the owner, who may or may not want a picture on their building. And there are the people who live with it every day.

These do not always agree. A legal mural can be resented by a whole street. An illegal one can be loved.

The case for not asking

The French artist JR has been frank about which permission matters to him. He told Elephant magazine that if the people in a place agree to a project, an official in an office should not be able to stop it, and that this is why much of his work is done illegally.

His project in Rio de Janeiro in 2008 shows the idea at its limit. To paste portraits of local women across a hillside favela, he did not go to the city. He went to the people who actually controlled the area, explained what he wanted to do and was allowed in.

The same artist, invited

Eight years later JR was back in Rio with full official blessing. For the 2016 Olympics he made The Giants, enormous images of athletes fitted to particular buildings. Same city, same artist, opposite arrangement.

That is the usual path. Vhils began as a teenage graffiti writer and now carves walls at festivals and on commission. Zen Xander is described as moving from the street to the gallery. Yesterday's trespass becomes today's invitation.

The uninvited guest

Then there is the artist who turns up at someone else's event. In October 2025, Konn Artiss left five blocks of ice outside the entrance to Frieze London, each holding a small figure of a famous artist. The published reports do not say whether anyone gave permission. The title, ANTI-FRIEZE, suggests what the answer would have been.

The work only makes sense in that spot. Move it to a gallery and it is a sculpture. Outside the fair, uninvited, it is a comment.

What permission changes

Permission is not neutral. A commissioned mural has been approved, which means someone decided it was safe. That is no insult to the work, but it shapes what can be said.

Work made without asking carries a risk that the commissioned kind does not, and viewers can feel it. The risk is also real for other people: the owner who has to clean a wall, the resident who never wanted a giant face outside the window.

A better question

So who owns the street? In law, the answer is clear enough. In practice, the street belongs to whoever uses it, and public art is one long argument about that.

The fairest test may be the one JR points to. Do the people who live with the work want it there? If they do, the paperwork tends to follow. If they do not, no amount of official approval will make it theirs.
